Time blindness: practical ways to see time again

By Jahnvi Aghera · 11 October 2026

You sit down at 10 to answer one email. You look up and it's 3:47. "Time blindness" is the name many people with ADHD use for this: trouble sensing how much time has passed or how long something will take. The fix usually isn't trying harder to feel time. It's making time visible from the outside, so you don't have to feel it.

Here are seven practical ways to do that.

1. Put a clock where your eyes already are

If you have to go and look for the time, you won't. Put a clock in your line of sight: next to your monitor, on the lock screen, or as a home-screen widget showing what's now and what's next.

2. Turn tasks into blocks with a start and an end

"Answer emails" has no edges. "Answer emails, 10:00–10:30" does. Giving every task a length turns a to-do list into a timeline you can see slipping, which is exactly the signal time blindness takes away.

3. Use a timer you can see from across the room

A small countdown in the corner is easy to tune out. A big, full-screen timer is hard to ignore, and seeing time run down is easier than guessing how much is left.

4. Ask for a second reminder, not just one

One notification is easy to swipe away mid-focus. A follow-up nudge when you still haven't started catches the moment the first one missed.

5. Add buffers between things

Most of us underestimate how long things take, especially transitions like getting ready or switching tasks. Add 10–15 minutes between blocks. If you don't need it, you've earned a break.

6. Get it out of your head first

A head full of half-remembered tasks makes it harder to notice time at all. Do a quick brain dump of everything on your mind, then put only today's few things on the timeline.

7. Make routines one tap, not a fresh decision

Mornings and wind-downs go smoother when the steps are already laid out with times. Build the routine once and reuse it instead of planning it from scratch every day.
